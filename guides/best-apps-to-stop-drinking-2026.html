The Best Apps to Stop Drinking in 2026

By the Sobermark team · Updated August 2026

People arrive at "I should drink less" from very different places. Some want to stop entirely, today. Some want to understand why they drink before changing it. Some want to cut down to weekends and see how that feels. The right app depends on which of those people you are, and a mismatch is the main reason drinking apps get deleted in week one.

Full disclosure: Sobermark is our app, so we are not neutral. We have tried to keep everything below fair, and we tell you when a rival is the better fit.

Here are six apps that take genuinely different approaches to alcohol, so at least one of them should match the change you actually want to make.

Reframe

Reframe treats changing your drinking as something to learn, not just to endure. It is a neuroscience-based programme with daily course content: short readings and exercises about why alcohol has the grip it does and how habits rewire. You work through it like a curriculum, a little each day, on iOS or Android.

It is subscription based, so check their store listing for current pricing. Reframe is the pick if you want to be taught, and if you know you stick with things that give you homework. If you already understand your drinking and just want to track not doing it, a programme this involved can feel like wading.

Sobermark

Sobermark is our app, so read this with that in mind. It sits at the simple end: a free Android tracker with a day counter, and money saved, time reclaimed and units avoided calculated from what you actually drank and spent. For the moments that decide whether day 12 becomes day 13, there are craving SOS tools, a hold-on breathing exercise and a ride-it-out timer, plus a journal with moods so you can spot your own patterns.

There is no account, no sign up and no feed; your entries stay on your phone. A slip resets the counter without a lecture, and Bern the sun bear keeps things kind. If drinking is not your only battle, it tracks smoking, vaping, weed, gambling or a custom habit alongside. Sobermark gives every new user a 3 day free trial, then costs £14.99 a year, £2.99 a week, or £24.99 once for lifetime (lifetime is Google Play only). It is on both Google Play and the App Store. If you want education or coaching rather than tracking, Reframe or Sunnyside will serve you better.

Sunnyside

Sunnyside is honest about something most sobriety apps are not built for: many people do not want to quit, they want to drink less. It is a mindful drinking tracker where you set a weekly plan, log your drinks, and get supportive text-based coaching along the way. There is no abstinence assumption and no recovery language.

It is subscription based; check the current price on their site or store listing. If moderation is your genuine goal, Sunnyside is the best fit on this list. If you have tried moderation repeatedly and it keeps not working, an abstinence-based tool may be the kinder choice.

Try Dry

Try Dry is the free official app of Alcohol Change UK, the charity behind Dry January. You log drinks and dry days, watch units, calories and money add up, and take on challenges like a dry month. It handles both cutting down and stopping, and because a charity runs it, the app has no commercial reason to keep you hooked on it.

It is the obvious starting point for a Dry January or Sober October, and a good low-pressure first step if the word "sobriety" feels too big yet. People who want daily motivation or craving tools will find it fairly quiet.

Drink Less

Drink Less comes from researchers at University College London, and it is one of the few drinking apps actually studied in trials. It is free, on iOS, and focuses on evidence-backed techniques for cutting down: goal setting, self-monitoring, feedback on your drinking versus norms. It is unflashy in the way research tools tend to be, and all the better for it.

If you are an iPhone user who wants something free with science behind it rather than a subscription with marketing behind it, start here. Android users will need to look elsewhere on this list, and anyone wanting warmth or personality will find it clinical.

I Am Sober

I Am Sober rounds out the list as the strongest community option. Its daily pledge, milestone system and large user base make it feel like quitting alongside others rather than alone, and reading how strangers handled week two while you are in week two is genuinely steadying. It runs on iOS and Android with a usable free tier and a subscription on top; check the store listing for current details.

It is built around abstinence, so it suits stopping rather than moderating. Choose it if other people's stories keep you going. If sharing feels like pressure, a private tracker will treat you more gently.

Who should pick what

Want to learn your way out of drinking? Reframe. Want to moderate, not quit? Sunnyside. Doing a dry month or easing in? Try Dry. On iPhone and want free, evidence-based help cutting down? Drink Less. Want company and accountability? I Am Sober.

Pick Sobermark if you have decided to stop and you want a private, calm counter with craving tools and no subscription required for the core.

Common questions

Should I aim to quit or cut down?

That depends on your health, your history and your honest read of yourself, and it is a good question for a doctor rather than an app. As a rough rule, if moderation attempts keep failing, that is information.

Is it safe to just stop drinking?

For heavy, daily drinkers, stopping suddenly can be dangerous and can need medical supervision. If your body is used to alcohol every day, talk to a GP before you stop. This is one area where an app is the wrong tool for the first step.

Do I need to pay for a good drinking app?

No. Try Dry, Drink Less and Sobermark's core are free. Paid apps like Reframe and Sunnyside buy you structure and coaching, which is worth it for some people and unnecessary for others.

What actually happens to cravings over time?

For most people they get shorter, rarer and less convincing, especially after the first few weeks. Tools that help you sit through the ten worst minutes, whatever app they are in, are the ones that matter.

No app is medical advice or a substitute for professional help. If stopping could affect your health, or things feel too heavy, speak to a doctor. Free, confidential helplines for every country are listed at findahelpline.com.
